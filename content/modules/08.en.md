## 01 · The k-way objective

Assign every vertex to one of k disjoint, nonempty parts. In an ordinary graph, an edge contributes its weight once whenever endpoint labels differ. Increasing k does not multiply that charge. Meaningful comparison requires the same input, objective and capacity.

We compare input-order round robin, largest-part recursive spectral bisection, and recursive construction followed by capacity-legal swaps. The last is called swap refinement, not full k-way FM: its neighborhood and acceptance rules differ.

## 02 · A round-robin baseline

Assign input vertex i to i mod k. At k=3, G01 becomes AD | BE | C. Every edge crosses, so cut=18. Counts 2/2/1 are even, but the method ignores weights. Balanced counts and good connections are separate properties.

The baseline depends on input order. Comparisons preserve that order. Sorting vertices first would define a different method and should be stated rather than silently used to improve a result.

## 03 · Recursive construction

Recursive splitting gives DE | ABC, then AB | C, yielding AB | C | DE with cut 8. It preserves the heavy AB and DE edges. Counts are 2/1/2, so unit areas satisfy capacity 2.

Increasing A's area can invalidate the same grouping. The constructor does not automatically account for final capacity. Check feasibility before refinement and report failure instead of hiding an illegal start behind a low objective.

## 04 · Cross-part swap refinement

Enumerate pairs in different parts. Hypothetically exchange their labels, check every area against capacity, then compute gain=cut_before−cut_after. Accept the largest strictly positive legal gain, breaking ties by vertex names. Repeat until none exists or the step limit is reached.

Swaps preserve counts but can change areas. This neighborhood contains neither single-node moves nor a full pass of negative-gain trials and best-prefix rollback. It can stop even when another neighborhood could improve the solution.

Default G01 at k=3 and capacity 2 has no improving swap from cut 8. The correct trace therefore stays at 8. A useful explanation can show why nothing improves; it should not invent motion to make an animation busier.

## 05 · Calibrate with an exact tiny solver

The oracle enumerates legal nonempty assignments while removing duplicate part-label permutations. For G01, k=3 and capacity 2, its optimum is 8. Agreement proves optimality only for that instance.

The browser oracle supports at most 9 vertices and 4 parts because the search is exponential. Larger teaching inputs can still run heuristics, while exact comparison is explicitly skipped. An uncomputed optimum must not appear as zero.

## 06 · Change a weight and rerun

Change AB from 5 to 1 at k=3. Round robin still cuts every edge, reducing its cost from 18 to 14. The spectral and recursive methods solve again and can produce A | BC | DE with cut 5. Inspect actual events; ties or repeated eigenvalues may permit equivalent groupings.

This checks whether the method responds to its input. A canned sequence cannot support the same experiment. Also vary capacity, sizes and k to separate objective changes from feasibility changes.

## 07 · Report a reproducible comparison

Record the graph, vertex order, sizes, k, capacity, construction strategy, ties, stop reason, final cut and part areas. Do not rank infeasible rows alongside feasible ones by cost. Report an optimality gap only when an exact result is available.
