## 01 · Check the assignment first

Represent circuit cells by vertices and connections by undirected edges. An edge weight models the cost of placing its endpoints in different regions. This is a deliberately simple model: it does not predict complete routed wire length or timing. It does let us compare assignments under a clearly defined objective.

A bisection assigns every vertex exactly once and leaves both sides nonempty. Omitting C, placing C on both sides, or putting everything on the left is invalid here. Validate before measuring. Otherwise a missing label can accidentally behave like an extra part and produce a plausible but meaningless number.

The shared graph G01 has vertices A–E and edges AB:5, AC:1, BC:4, CD:2, CE:1 and DE:5. Every edge appears once and every vertex initially has size 1. The later lessons reuse this graph so that differences in algorithms are visible without changing the input.

## 02 · Count each crossing edge once

The objective is cut = Σ w(u,v)·[part(u) ≠ part(v)]. The bracket is 1 when its condition is true and 0 otherwise. Endpoint labels determine whether an edge crosses. Its position on the screen, or an intersection with another drawn line, has no effect.

For AE | BCD, the crossing edges are AB, AC, CE and DE: 5+1+1+5 = 12. CD does not cross because both endpoints are on the right. For ABC | DE, only CD and CE cross, giving 2+1 = 3. Both assignments have side counts 2 and 3, yet their costs differ by 9. Equal balance does not mean equal connection quality.

## 03 · Counts, areas and capacities

When every size equals 1, counts and areas coincide. Change A's size to 4: ABC still contains three vertices, but its area becomes 6; DE has count and area 2. Under capacity 4, ABC | DE has a low cut of 3 but is infeasible.

The course reports counts and areas separately. KL exchanges one vertex from each side, preserving counts; unequal vertex sizes can still change the areas. The basic FM lab also constrains count difference. The k-way capacity parameter instead constrains area. A balance claim needs both its measurement and its threshold.

Total area S and capacity C imply at least ceil(S/C) parts. This is necessary, not sufficient. Three vertices of area 3 cannot fit into two parts of capacity 5, even though total capacity 10 exceeds total area 9: no pair of these vertices can share a part.

## 04 · Two balance summaries

For side areas s0 and s1, ratio = min(s0,s1)/max(s0,s1), where values closer to 1 are more even. Imbalance = |s0−s1|/(s0+s1), where values closer to 0 are more even. For areas 2 and 3 the values are 2/3 and 1/5. Their thresholds are not interchangeable.

An empty side has ratio 0, but is still infeasible. The evaluator returns numerical diagnostics alongside nonempty and feasible flags. This prevents an all-on-one-side assignment with cut 0 from looking like a successful answer.

For k parts, the interface shows every area plus the minimum/maximum ratio and range/total summary. These are descriptive teaching metrics, not an unstated normalized-cut objective. Compare results only after aligning k, sizes and capacity.

## 05 · Feasibility precedes optimization

An optimization problem has an objective and constraints. Here the objective minimizes cut; constraints require a complete, disjoint, nonempty assignment and the declared balance or capacity. Filter for legality before comparing costs. Mixing illegal and legal assignments rewards avoiding the problem's rules.

If legal assignments tie on cut, report their distributions without silently adding a new objective. Later engines also state tie rules, such as vertex-name order. Those rules make experiments, challenge answers and tests reproducible.

This lesson evaluates assignments; it does not automatically optimize them. You choose each move in the lab. Later lessons introduce algorithmic decisions. A tiny exact oracle can calibrate an example, but does not establish a general optimality guarantee for a heuristic.

## 06 · Implement a reliable evaluator

Validate unique vertex names, positive sizes, existing endpoints and finite weights. Then verify that the assignment supplies a valid part for every vertex. An adjacency list often stores each undirected edge twice. Summing it directly double-counts; the normalized edge list used here stores each edge once.

```python
cost = 0
for u, v, w in edges:
    if assignment[u] != assignment[v]:
        cost += w
```

Test properties, not just the remembered answer 3. Renaming the parts leaves cut unchanged. Multiplying every weight by c multiplies cut by c. Increasing one crossing edge by Δ increases cut by exactly Δ. These checks expose errors that a single fixture can miss.
